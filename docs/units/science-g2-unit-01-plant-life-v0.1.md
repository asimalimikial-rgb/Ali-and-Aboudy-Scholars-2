## Unit overview

**Big idea:** Plants are living things. We use different parts of plants, and seeds can grow into new plants when conditions are suitable.

**Unit outcomes**
By the end of this unit, the learner should be able to:
1. Identify plants as living things using observable evidence.
2. Describe common plant parts and connect some parts to their functions.
3. Identify familiar foods and useful materials that come from plants.
4. Describe the early stages of seed growth and identify conditions that support healthy plant growth.
5. Observe, compare, classify, record, and explain simple findings using evidence.

**Vocabulary:** plant, living, grow, root, stem, leaf/leaves, flower, fruit, seed, soil, germinate, cotton, wood, observe, compare.

**Language policy:** English is the science learning language. Arabic helper text is a separate, optional, brief pre-answer aid; it is not a full Arabic translation of the lesson. ## Lesson L1 — Plants Are Living Things

**Learning intention:** I can identify a plant as a living thing and describe what I observe.

**Teach**
Plants are living things. A plant can grow and change. Many plants have roots, stems, and leaves. Plants need water and air. Most green plants use light to make their own food. Different plants can look very different: a small herb, a date palm, and a shade tree are all plants.

**Guided practice**
Show three original illustrations: a living potted plant, a wooden stool, and a plastic toy tree. Ask the learner to compare what each is made of and which one can grow.

**Independent practice**
Use a new set of examples. The learner identifies the living plant and selects an observable reason.

**Transfer**
Observe one plant in the home or outside with an adult. Record one feature today and one visible change later. If no plant is available, use an original sequence of plant photographs/illustrations.

**Misconceptions to diagnose**
- A wooden object is still living because it came from a tree.
- Every green-looking object is a living plant.
- Plants do not change because they do not walk.

**Optional Arabic helper (pre-answer only):** «اختَر النبات الحي، وفكّر: هل يمكنه أن ينمو؟»

## Lesson L2 — Parts of Plants and Plants We Eat

**Learning intention:** I can name common plant parts and identify plant parts that people eat.

**Teach**
Many familiar plants have roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, and seeds. Roots usually take in water from the soil and help hold a plant in place. Stems support the plant and move water to other parts. Leaves help many plants make food using light. People eat different plant parts: carrot roots, spinach leaves, celery stems, broccoli flowers, tomatoes as fruits, and peas as seeds.

**Guided practice**
Use one original labelled plant diagram. Then sort food illustrations by the part of the plant we eat. Explain that a food can be a plant part even when we do not usually see the whole plant.

**Independent practice**
Classify new examples by edible plant part. Use familiar foods but avoid assuming every child has access to every food.

**Transfer**
Create a simple food observation chart at home with an adult: food name, plant source if known, and plant part. “Not sure” is an acceptable answer.

**Misconceptions to diagnose**
- Roots are the part above the soil.
- All vegetables are leaves.
- A fruit is always sweet.
- A seed is not part of a plant.

## Lesson L3 — Useful Plants

**Learning intention:** I can connect familiar foods and materials to plants.

**Teach**
People use plants in many ways. We eat fruits, seeds, leaves, roots, and other plant parts. Cotton fibres come from cotton plants and can be made into cloth. Wood from trees can be used to make furniture and paper. Some plants provide materials, shade, and useful products. We should use plants carefully and avoid wasting them.

**Guided practice**
Show original illustrations of cotton cloth, a wooden chair, paper, dates, and lentils. Match each item to a plausible plant source. Make clear that processed products may look very different from the plant they came from.

**Independent practice**
Classify familiar items as plant-based or not plant-based, then identify the source for selected examples.

**Transfer**
Find one object made partly from a plant material with an adult. Describe the evidence or label; if the source is uncertain, record “not sure” rather than guessing.

**Misconceptions to diagnose**
- Paper grows as sheets on trees.
- Cotton cloth grows ready-made on a plant.
- Every useful material comes from plants.
- Processed foods no longer have a plant source.

**Optional Arabic helper (pre-answer only):** «من أي نبات أو جزء نباتي جاء هذا الشيء؟»

## Lesson L4 — Seeds and Plant Growth

**Learning intention:** I can describe how a seed begins to grow and identify conditions that help a young plant.

**Teach**
A seed can germinate: it begins to grow and produces a tiny root and shoot. Many seeds need water, air, and suitable warmth to begin germinating. After a seedling grows leaves, light helps the green plant make food. Soil can support many plants and provide water and minerals, but a seed does not need to be planted in soil to begin germinating. Gardeners care for plants by providing suitable conditions and checking them as they grow.

**Guided practice**
Use an original four-picture sequence: dry seed; seed swelling after water; tiny root emerging; young seedling with leaves. Ask the learner to order the stages and explain what changed.

**Independent practice**
Choose the condition needed in a simple scenario and interpret a small observation record. Do not treat one classroom observation as proof that all seeds behave identically.

**Transfer / safe investigation**
With an adult, place a few beans on damp paper in a clear container. Keep the paper damp, not flooded; observe once a day and draw or record visible changes. Do not eat the seeds used in the investigation. If materials are unavailable, use the illustrated sequence.

**Scientific accuracy note**
For this age level, distinguish germination from later seedling growth: water, air, and suitable warmth support germination for many common seeds; light becomes important for the green seedling as it develops. Do not teach “soil is always essential for germination” as a universal rule.

**Misconceptions to diagnose**
- A seed needs bright sunlight before it can begin germinating.
- A seed grows immediately into a flower.
- More water is always better.
- Soil is the only possible place for a seed to begin germinating.

**Optional Arabic helper (pre-answer only):** «رتّب مراحل نمو البذرة، وفكّر فيما تحتاجه في البداية.»

## Suggested lesson assessment

For each lesson:
1. One diagnostic item before teaching.
2. Two guided-practice items with immediate feedback.
3. Three independent items using varied examples.
4. One transfer item in a new context.
5. One delayed retrieval item in a later session.

Do not interpret a correct response after a hint as independent mastery. A single correct answer is not enough to mark a skill mastered. Use evidence across more than one item form and more than one session; calibrate numeric thresholds only after enough item-response data is available.
