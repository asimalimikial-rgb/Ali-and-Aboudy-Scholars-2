## 1. Unit overview

**Big idea:** Animals are living things with different body features, needs and life cycles. Their features and behaviours can help them live in particular habitats.

### Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, the learner should be able to:
1. Identify animals as living things using age-appropriate evidence.
2. Match familiar young animals with adults and recognise that animals have different life cycles.
3. Group familiar animals as vertebrates or invertebrates using the presence or absence of a backbone.
4. Identify insects by their basic body plan and six legs.
5. Explain how a habitat and a visible feature or behaviour can help an animal meet its needs and survive.
6. Use observation, comparison, classification, sequencing and evidence-based explanation.

### Vocabulary

animal, living, young, adult, habitat, vertebrate, invertebrate, backbone, insect, antennae, head, thorax, abdomen, life cycle, egg, larva, pupa, tadpole, predator, prey, camouflage, feature, survive.

### Safety and language

- English is the science learning language.
- Any Arabic helper is brief, optional, separate and pre-answer only; it is not a translation of the lesson.
- Observe insects from a safe distance. Do not touch unknown insects, nests, eggs, wild animals or animal droppings.
- No handling, feeding or trapping of wild animals is required. Practical observation is adult-supervised.
- Do not teach that every animal has the same life cycle, that all insects have wings, or that camouflage makes an animal invisible in every environment.

## 3. Lesson L1 — What Makes an Animal Living?

**Learning intention:** I can identify an animal as a living thing and describe what it needs.

**Teach**

Animals are living things. Animals grow and need food, water and air. Animals respond to things around them. Different animals move in different ways: some walk, some swim, some fly, and some move very slowly. A toy animal may look like a real animal, but it does not grow or need food and water.

Animals live in habitats. A habitat is a place where an animal can find the things it needs, such as food, water, shelter and enough space. A camel, a fish and a frog do not all need the same habitat.

Many animals produce young of their own kind. Young animals have different names and may look similar to or very different from their adults.

**Guided practice:** compare an original picture set of a living goat, a toy goat, and a wooden animal carving. Then match kitten/cat, chick/hen and tadpole/frog. Discuss why the young and adult of some animals look different.

**Independent practice:** choose evidence of animal life and select a suitable habitat for a familiar animal.

**Transfer:** observe an animal from a safe distance with an adult. Record one visible feature and one need it may meet in its habitat. Do not approach or feed wild animals.

**Misconceptions to diagnose**
- An animal must walk to be alive.
- A toy animal is alive because it looks realistic.
- Every young animal is a smaller copy of its adult.
- Every animal can live in the same place.

## 4. Lesson L2 — Different Kinds of Animals

**Learning intention:** I can group familiar animals by whether they have a backbone.

**Teach**

Animals can be grouped in different ways. One useful science grouping is based on a backbone. Vertebrates have a backbone. Examples include fish, frogs, birds, reptiles and mammals. Invertebrates do not have a backbone. Examples include insects, snails, spiders and earthworms.

Do not classify animals by size, habitat or whether they move quickly. A small fish is a vertebrate; a large octopus is an invertebrate. A spider is an invertebrate but is not an insect.

**Guided practice:** use original cards to sort familiar animals into “has a backbone” and “does not have a backbone”. Ask the learner to explain the sorting rule.

**Independent practice:** classify new examples and compare animals with different body plans.

**Transfer:** observe animal pictures from two habitats and group them by the stated backbone rule, not by where they live.

**Misconceptions**
- All small animals are insects.
- Animals that live in water are all fish.
- An animal's number of legs tells whether it has a backbone.
- A spider is an insect.

## 5. Lesson L3 — Insects Around Us

**Learning intention:** I can identify an insect by its body features and observe it safely.

**Teach**

An adult insect has six legs and three main body regions: head, thorax and abdomen. Insects usually have a pair of antennae. Many adult insects have wings, but not all insects have wings. A spider is not an insect: it has eight legs and a different body plan.

Insects have different roles. Some bees and butterflies help pollinate flowers. Some insects can damage crops or sting. The learner should observe insects from a safe distance and never touch an unknown insect or nest.

**Guided practice:** count legs on clear original insect and spider illustrations; identify head, thorax and abdomen on a simplified diagram.

**Independent practice:** classify examples from body features, not size or fear.

**Transfer:** observe a photograph of an insect visiting a flower and explain one way the insect may help the plant. No live insect handling.

**Misconceptions**
- Every small creature is an insect.
- Wings are counted as legs.
- All insects have wings.
- Spiders are insects.
- Every insect is harmful or every insect is harmless.

## 6. Lesson L4 — Animal Life Cycles

**Learning intention:** I can order stages in familiar animal life cycles.

**Teach**

A life cycle shows how a living thing changes as it grows and develops. Different animals have different life cycles.

A butterfly's life cycle has four familiar stages: egg, caterpillar (larva), pupa (chrysalis), adult butterfly. A frog develops from egg to tadpole, then froglet and adult frog. A chicken develops from egg to chick and then adult chicken. These examples are not the same sequence.

A young animal grows and develops. Some young animals look similar to adults; others change shape more dramatically.

**Guided practice:** sequence original picture cards for butterfly, frog and chicken separately.

**Independent practice:** select the correct next stage and compare two life cycles.

**Transfer:** use a new sequence card set and explain one difference between the butterfly and chicken life cycles.

**Misconceptions**
- All animals hatch from eggs.
- A butterfly caterpillar is an adult butterfly.
- A tadpole is a small fish.
- All life cycles have the same stages.

## 7. Lesson L5 — How Animals Survive

**Learning intention:** I can explain how a body feature or behaviour can help an animal survive.

**Teach**

A predator is an animal that hunts another animal for food. The animal being hunted is called prey. The same animal may be a predator in one relationship and prey in another.

Camouflage is a feature or colour pattern that helps an animal blend into its surroundings. It can make an animal harder to see in a suitable habitat, but it does not make the animal invisible everywhere. Other body features can help animals find food, move, stay safe or cope with their habitat.

Examples should be explained with care:
- A stick insect's shape and colour can make it harder to notice among branches.
- A hare's coat may help it blend into a suitable environment.
- A duck's webbed feet help it push against water when swimming.
- A camel's broad feet help it walk on soft sand. Its hump stores fat, not water.

**Guided practice:** compare original pictures of an animal in a matching and non-matching background; identify predator and prey in a simple food relationship.

**Independent practice:** select the feature that helps in a stated habitat and explain why.

**Transfer:** compare the same animal feature in two different backgrounds and decide when it is most useful.

**Misconceptions**
- A predator is always the biggest animal.
- Camouflage makes an animal invisible in every place.
- A camel stores water in its hump.
- One adaptation is useful in every habitat.
- Prey animals do not have ways to avoid predators.

## 8. Unit assessment design

The 30 items are candidates, not a one-sitting quiz. Use a sequence:
1. Diagnostic: one item before instruction.
2. Guided: teacher/parent modelling, visual comparison and scaffolded practice.
3. Independent: at least two fresh items without semantic help.
4. Transfer: a changed animal, habitat or life-cycle example.
5. Delayed review: a new item in a later session.

A supported answer is evidence of learning-in-progress, not independent mastery. Use more than one item form and more than one session before judging a skill secure.

## 9. Question bank QA controls

- Each item has one intended answer and three unique choices.
- Distractors correspond to a documented misconception.
- Avoid distractors that are obviously silly or unrelated.
- Use “many”, “usually”, “can” and “in this example” where biology varies.
- Avoid ambiguous common names where the body plan is the target.
- Use accessible diagrams with clear body-region labels; no textbook artwork.
- Do not require children to read dense text to demonstrate a science concept.
- For life-cycle ordering, use ORDER_SEQUENCE only after the renderer is implemented; the current app renderer supports SINGLE_CHOICE only.
- Per-option feedback and misconception capture require the proposed v4 content model; do not add unsupported JSON fields to v3.
